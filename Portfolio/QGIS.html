Lethality Assessment for North Carolina Bicycle Infrastructure

This comprehensive analysis that showcases a logisitic Regresion Model which measures the lethality probability of specific factors that will be seen when Biking.

Key Findings:

  • Sidewalks cut severe crash odds by 69% (OR = 0.31), the strongest protective effect.
  • Unlit roads double severe crash risk (OR = 2.19), emphasizing lighting investments.
  • Fog increases severity 2.5×, but rare occurrences limit generalizability.
  • Coarse asphalt raises severity 18%, highlighting pavement quality as a modifiable factor.

Lethality Assessment for North Carolina Bicycle Infrastructure

Crash Rate Per Mile by Infrastructure Type

Key Findings:

  • Travel lanes account for 61.7% of crashes, reflecting their prevalence and higher exposure.
  • Multi-use paths have highest crash rate (1.84/mile), but PBIN underreporting may skew results.
  • Sidewalks safest per mile (0.16 crashes), yet urban bias in data may overstate their efficacy.
  • Bike lanes (0.4/mile) are middling, supporting their role as a compromise between safety and utility.

Lethality Assessment for North Carolina Bicycle Infrastructure

Proportion of Total Crashes by Infrastructure.

Key Findings:

  • Travel lanes account for 61.7% of crashes, reflecting their prevalence and higher exposure.

  • Sidewalk/Crosswalk had the highest proportion of low-severity crashes (12.0%).

Binary Logistic Model: Severe Crash Probability by Infrastructure.

Lethality Assessment for North Carolina Bicycle Infrastructure

Interaction: Infrastructure × Road Surface.

Key Findings:

  • Coarse asphalt increases severe crash risk across all infrastructure types, peaking on travel lanes (5% vs. 4% for smooth asphalt).
  • Road surface quality is a modifiable factor—smoother pavements could reduce severity, especially on high-risk roads.

Lethality Assessment for North Carolina Bicycle Infrastructure

Interaction: Infrastructure × Lighting.

Key Findings:

  • Unlit roads at night doubles severe crash risk (8.5% probability on travel lanes vs. 4% in daylight).
  • Sidewalks are least affected by lighting (severe crashes rise only 1–2% in darkness), likely due to lower vehicle interactions.
  • This of severity is similar among all forms of infrastructure, showing that light is a universal factor in lowering severity.
  • Dusk poses unique risks, with severity spikes suggesting transitional lighting conditions are critical intervention points.

Lethality Assessment for North Carolina Bicycle Infrastructure

Interaction: Infrastructure × Road Classification.

Key Findings:

  • NC/US routes are deadliest for all infrastructure (e.g., 9-10% severe crashes on travel lanes), emphasizing speed/design risks.
  • Local streets + sidewalks = safest combo (2% severe), validating low-speed urban corridors.
  • Secondary routes are universally hazardous (2.5-8% severe crashes).
  • Infrastructure benefits diminish on high-order roads, urging redesign (e.g., lowered speed limits, protected lanes).

Lethality Assessment for North Carolina Bicycle Infrastructure

Interaction: Infrastructure × Weather.

Key Findings:

  • Fog is the most dangerous condition, doubling severe crash risk on travel lanes (10% vs. 4% in clear weather).
  • Sidewalks remain resilient in bad weather, with only a 2-3% severity increase during fog/rain.
  • Rain’s impact is negligible after controlling for other factors, contradicting anecdotal assumptions.
  • Weather-infrastructure interactions are could be impacted by sample size, with fog being a less common event for bikers.

NIMBY Factors in Charlotte Analysis

Analysis of the phrasings and messaging of Charlotte's NIMBY movement and its relationship with property

Key Findings:

  • "Traffic" dominates mentions, peaking in 2022, reflecting its centrality in NIMBY opposition to development.
  • "Density" shows significant spikes, aligning with debates over high-density vs. low-density housing proposals.
  • "Property value" is the least mentioned term, suggesting economic self-interest may be downplayed in public discourse.
  • Opposition to development increasingly relies on strategically framed terms like “character” and “density” over “property value” to signal collective concern, with “traffic” serving as a versatile but less targeted critique, and a notable spike in such language in 2022 suggesting responses to post-pandemic pressures or key policy shifts.

NIMBY Factors in Charlotte Analysis

Analysis of the phrasings and messaging of Charlotte's NIMBY movement and its relationship with property.

Key Findings:

  • "Character" has the strongest NIMBY link: 85.2% of mentions tied to opposing development to preserve neighborhood aesthetics.
  • "Property value" is highly targeted (second-highest housing association) but rarely used, hinting at strategic avoidance of overt self-interest.
  • "Noise" is least related to housing, often referencing complaints about events or microphone feedback in meetings.
  • Policymakers should explicitly address concerns about “character” and “density” in housing proposals, distinguish true development-related traffic issues from general municipal complaints, conduct regional comparisons to account for local framing differences (e.g., Charlotte vs. San Francisco), and improve data granularity by tracking exact phrases and linking them to project outcomes.

Charlotte Transportation Study

Using a Regression to find the relationship between Transportation and Property Value.

Key Findings:

  • Figure 1: Scatter Plot - Visual shows a weak inverse trend—properties closer to transit hubs tend to have slightly higher values, but with heavy dispersion.
  • Figure 2: Simple Linear Regression - R² = 0.0193, indicating a very weak explanatory power. Highly significant p-value (2.2e-16) due to large sample size (n = 344,690), but low practical relevance.
  • Figure 3: Multiple Linear Regression - Adjusted R² = 0.1028, showing an improvement but still a weak model.
  • Weak correlation between distance to public transportation and normalized property value. Multiple regression improved the model but still failed to show strong predictive power. Results suggest other factors are likely more important determinants of property value.

Charlotte Transportation Study

Mean Change in Property Tax / Value based on Distance. Full=Full Dataset, Half = Half Mile, Quarter = Quarter Mile, Rest = Outside Half Mile.

Key Findings:

  • Proximity to transit (within 0.25 miles) correlates with ~5% higher property tax growth compared to areas farther away, indicating a positive but modest impact.
  • Suburban areas outside transit buffers also saw significant growth, driven by rising single-family home demand, suggesting transit is not the sole factor in value appreciation.
  • Economic events (2008 crash, COVID-19) had muted effects on transit-adjacent properties, highlighting their resilience compared to broader market trends.
  • Regression analysis found no strong direct link between distance to transit and property values, implying other urban factors (schools, zoning, amenities) play a larger role.

Bike Crash Density

An ArcGIS Kernel Density .

Image Highlights:

  • Urban clusters dominate - Highest crash density in Charlotte, Raleigh, and Durham metro areas
  • University hotspots visible - Distinct clusters near Chapel Hill (UNC) and Boone (App State) campuses
  • Density ≠ severity - Urban areas show more crashes but fewer severe injuries
  • Rural blind spot - Low crash density masks high severity risks on rural roads

Bike Facility Density

An ArcGIS Kernel Density

Image Highlights:

  • Facilities cluster in mid-sized cities (Goldsboro, Kinston) rather than largest metros, suggesting historical planning priorities over demand-driven allocation.
  • Density-Crash Paradox: Urban cores have high crash density but low severity, implying more cyclists + infrastructure = more minor incidents but fewer fatalities.
  • Rural "deserts" evident: 78% of PBIN mileage is in urban counties, leaving rural cyclists vulnerable - consistent with 20% severe crash rates on rural NC routes
  • Data caveat: Crowdsourced PBIN likely undercounts informal paths (e.g., farm roads), skewing rural facility estimates downward.

Hot Spot Analysis: All Injuries

An ArcGIS Hotspot Analysis.

Image Highlights:

  • Expanded hotspots include urban periphery counties (e.g., Forsyth, Alamance), revealing that moderate/low-severity crashes concentrate where suburban sprawl meets inadequate infrastructure.
  • Robeson County remains a hotspot, confirming its outlier status for both crash frequency and severity, likely tied to its underinvestment in bike/ped facilities.
  • Urban cores show neutral/no significance, reflecting the protective effect of traffic-calming measures and grid networks common in cities like Charlotte.
  • Data limitation: Police underreporting of minor crashes may artificially suppress urban hotspot detection.

Hot Spot Analysis: Severe Injuries Only

An ArcGIS Hotspot Analysis.

Image Highlights:

  • Rural counties dominate severe crash hotspots, with Robeson, Johnston, and Gates counties showing statistically significant clustering (99% confidence) - aligning with findings that high-speed rural roads (e.g., NC/US Routes) have 2.6× higher severe crash odds.
  • Urban "cold spots" (Charlotte/Raleigh) correlate with dense sidewalk/bike lane networks, supporting regression results where sidewalks reduce severe crash risk by 69%.
  • Spatial mismatch with crash density: Rural areas have fewer total crashes but higher severity, suggesting infrastructure gaps (e.g., lack of lighting, paved shoulders) exacerbate outcomes.
  • Policy implication: Targeted infrastructure upgrades (e.g., rumble strips, lighting) on rural highways could mitigate severity disparities.
